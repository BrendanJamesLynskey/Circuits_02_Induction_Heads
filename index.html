01

Recap: What Is an Induction Head?

Olsson, Elhage, Nanda, Joseph, DasSarma, Henighan et al. — Anthropic — March 2022 Transformer Circuits transformer-circuits.pub

The problem

Presentation 01's Mathematical Framework showed that two-layer attention-only transformers contain a specific, identifiable circuit: the induction head. This paper takes that toy-model object and asks the much bigger question: is this little circuit actually responsible for in-context learning in real, large models?

The contribution

An induction head implements a simple, almost algorithmic rule on a repeated sequence. Given context … [A][B] … [A] — where the current token is a second occurrence of [A] — it predicts [B]: "the last time I saw this token, what came next? Predict that again." It does this in two steps that two attention heads in adjacent layers compose to perform:

  • Prefix matching: the head attends back to earlier tokens that were preceded by the current token (or recent tokens) — i.e. it finds the previous [A] and looks at the token after it.
  • Copying: it then increases the logit of that attended-to token, raising [B]'s probability of being emitted next.

Crucially this needs two layers. A "previous-token head" in layer 1 writes each token's predecessor into the residual stream; the induction head in layer 2 uses that to match on prefixes. One-layer models cannot form induction heads at all — the composition is structurally impossible.

The insight

An induction head is not pattern-matching against a stored table of n-grams. It decouples the matching token [A] from the copied token [B], so it can complete patterns it has never seen during training — including on random, out-of-distribution sequences. That generality is the whole reason it can power in-context learning rather than mere memorization.

The mechanism

A head qualifies as an induction head if it does both things: prefix matching (attend to tokens that were followed by the current/recent token) and copying (increase the logit of the attended-to token). The authors characterised this behaviour across 34 transformers using more than 50,000 attention-head ablations — this is a measured, mechanistic claim, not a metaphor.

03

The Phase Change: A Kink in the Loss Curve

The observation

Induction heads do not fade in gradually. Early in training — in every transformer with more than one layer, of any size — there is a narrow window where induction heads abruptly crystallise. Three things happen at once in that window: a visible bump / kink appears in the training loss curve, induction heads form, and the model's in-context learning ability jumps sharply.

Measuring it: the in-context learning score

To quantify in-context learning, the authors define a simple heuristic. Take the loss on the 500th token in a context and subtract the loss on the 50th token, averaged over examples. If a model uses earlier context to predict later tokens better, this difference is large and negative. The indices 50 and 500 are "somewhat arbitrary" — just an early vs late probe. This score improves abruptly exactly at the phase change.

Why it matters

This is a clean, concrete example of a capability emerging via a discrete circuit forming, not a smooth slide down a loss curve. The synchronised timing — loss kink, induction-head formation, ICL jump — is the paper's first and most visible argument that the three are causally linked. It also makes the phase change a candidate early-warning signal for capability emergence, which is why it is of interest to safety.

04

Six Lines of Evidence: Rigorous to Circumstantial

No single experiment proves that induction heads cause in-context learning, so the paper stacks six independent arguments. The first five are rigorous in small attention-only models; the sixth is the circumstantial bridge that extends the story to large models.

#ArgumentWhat it showsStrength
1Macroscopic co-occurrenceThe phase change, induction-head formation and the ICL-score jump all happen in the same window.rigorous (small)
2Macroscopic co-perturbationArchitecture tweaks that shift when induction heads can form shift the loss kink and ICL jump by the same amount.rigorous (small)
3Direct ablationKnocking out induction heads at test time significantly reduces in-context learning.rigorous (small)
4Examples of generalityThe same heads defined for literal copying empirically do translation, pattern-matching and abstract tasks.suggestive
5Mechanistic plausibilityIn small models the circuit is transparent and extends naturally to richer ICL by reuse.suggestive
6ContinuityMetrics tied to induction heads and to ICL vary smoothly from small to large models.circumstantial (large)

The honest framing matters: arguments 1–3 establish causation in small attention-only models where the circuit can be fully dissected; arguments 4–6 carry the claim toward large models without ever fully closing it. Where they cannot dissect a large model, they show that its behaviour lies on the same continuum as the small ones they can.

Caveat

The authors are explicit that this is "only the beginnings of evidence" and that "a large number of subtle confounds or alternative hypotheses are possible." The strong claim — induction heads are the majority mechanism of in-context learning in large models — remains a hypothesis supported by converging evidence, not a closed proof.

05

Generalization: "Do the Analogous Thing"

If induction heads only copied exact repeats, they could not explain most in-context learning. The key finding is that the same heads, defined narrowly on literal copying, empirically implement a far more abstract operation: fuzzy, nearest-neighbour pattern completion, written [A*][B*] … [A] → [B] where A* ≈ A and B* ≈ B.

Literal copy

[A][B] … [A] → [B]

Exact repetition. The defining behaviour — and the easy case to verify mechanistically.

Fuzzy / soft induction

[A*][B*] … [A] → [B]

Match on tokens that are merely similar, not identical. Nearest-neighbour completion over the embedding space.

Abstract / translation

chat cat … chien → dog

The analogy generalises across languages and tasks — translation, pattern completion, "continue this structure."

Because the circuit decouples the matched token from the copied token, it never collapses into a fixed n-gram table. That decoupling is what lets it operate on novel and out-of-distribution sequences, and what makes "increase the probability of the analogous continuation" a plausible description of what a large fraction of attention heads are doing when you give a model examples in its prompt.

The insight

The narrow definition ("literal copy") and the broad behaviour ("do the analogous thing") are the same heads. That is precisely why the authors find it plausible that induction heads explain not just clever sequence-copying but the bulk of few-shot, in-context learning — the abstract version is the literal version run on soft, similarity-based matches.

06

Why It Matters to a Practising Engineer

This paper is the mechanistic answer to a question you rely on every day without thinking about it: why does putting examples in a prompt work at all?

  • Few-shot prompting has a circuit. When you paste worked examples into a prompt, you are feeding the [A][B] … [A] structure that induction heads consume. "The model learns from the examples" is, mechanistically, induction heads doing soft prefix-match-and-copy in a single forward pass — no weights change.
  • Long contexts help for a reason. The in-context learning score — later tokens predicted better than early ones — is the measurable footprint of the model exploiting what is already in the window. More relevant context literally gives induction heads more to match against.
  • Capabilities can appear discretely. The phase change is a worked example of an ability switching on over a narrow training window rather than emerging smoothly. When you see a "sudden" new capability at scale, a discrete circuit forming is a real candidate explanation.
  • Interpretability is becoming measurable. 34 models, 50,000+ ablations, a clean scalar score — this is mechanistic interpretability done at the scale and rigour of an engineering result, not a thought experiment.
Why it matters

The next time a prompt "just works" because you added two good examples, you now know the name of the thing doing the work, what shape of input it needs, and the single training event that gave the model the ability in the first place.

07

What to Take Away

  • Induction heads are a concrete two-layer circuit: a previous-token head plus a head that does prefix matching then copying, completing [A][B] … [A] → [B]. They require more than one layer to exist.
  • The central hypothesis: induction heads are the primary mechanism behind in-context learning — not just in toy models, but plausibly the majority of ICL in transformers of all sizes.
  • The phase change is real and sharp: a narrow training window where induction heads form, a bump/kink appears in the loss, and the in-context learning score (loss at token 500 minus token 50) jumps — all at once.
  • The case is built from six converging arguments, rigorous via ablation and co-perturbation in small attention-only models, circumstantial (continuity) in large ones — and the authors are candid about the remaining gap.
  • Induction heads generalise from literal copying to fuzzy matching, soft induction, translation and pattern completion — an abstract "do the analogous thing" operation, enabled by decoupling the matched token from the copied one.
  • For engineers, this is the mechanistic story of why few-shot prompting and long contexts work, and a clean case study in capabilities emerging via discrete circuits.
Where to next
